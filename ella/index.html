GRAVID-AMEN: Building, Deploying and Handing Over a Maternal Health Application

Ella Thorburn

Honours in Computer Science, University of Cape Town, 2026
Supervisor: A/Prof Melissa Densmore

Download the paper (PDF) Source code (ZIP)

Ella's part of the project

  • Development of the app and the content dashboard
  • Deployment to phones at three clinics
  • Handover to the University of Limpopo AMEN team

Field results are on Sarah-Jane Marais's page.

Summary

Ella built GRAVID-AMEN, an offline-first maternal nutrition app for rural Limpopo, through a field build and a post-fieldwork build, then handed it over to a developer employed by the University of Limpopo AMEN team. The receiving developer's build was used in a wider pilot in August.

The paper finds that documented, runnable code is not enough for continuity. Access to project artefacts has to stay with the project, recorded reasoning has to reach the receiving developer, and maintenance notices have to reach someone responsible and able to act.

Aims

1

Aim 1: Design and development

Build the app against the product requirements, first to a version fit for field work, then to one ready for continued development and deployment.

2

Aim 2: Maintainability

Build the app so a receiving developer can set it up, understand it and iterate on it.

Three builds

  1. 1

    Field build

    Fully offline, with content bundled into the app. Used at three clinics.

  2. 2

    Post-fieldwork build

    Remote backend, download on demand, synchronisation, a content dashboard and dependency monitoring.

  3. 3

    Pilot build

    Written by the receiving developer at the University of Limpopo and used in the August pilot.

Requirements analysis

The inherited product requirements document was only partly adapted from the Nigerian GRAVID app and contained contradictions, such as naming Swahili as the second language for a Sepedi-speaking region. Ella audited it against the users' realities and produced four sets of requirements.

Two requirements rejected

Phone-number registration with an SMS one-time passcode

It needs a connection at first launch, and the app must work offline. The app signs users in anonymously instead.

A 2 MB limit per content bundle

Audio and video of usable quality are seldom under 2 MB.

The maintainability requirements were drawn from two earlier UCT HCI Lab projects (Milk Matters and the Bhabhisana Baby Project), the literature, and interviews with people from those projects and the AMEN project lead.

The field build

Content as data

Content items carry attributes such as language, media type, tags, and which roles and pregnancy stage they are relevant for, so screens render content from data.

One content repository

Every screen reads content through a single content repository, so serving content from a remote source could be added later.

Two languages, one entry

Sepedi and English versions of an item share a canonical identifier and appear as one entry. Interface text switches language without a restart and is worded for the user's role: a family member and a pregnant woman read the same fact addressed differently.

Shared phones

The app supports multiple profiles on one phone, because phones are often shared.

A familiar layout

The layout follows WhatsApp's conventions, the platform participants already used: five-item bottom navigation, content rows with a thumbnail and title, filter chips, and settings rows with an icon and chevron.

The 24 USB-captured devices came from six manufacturers and at least eighteen models, all budget or entry-level. Two installations failed for lack of storage space.

A familiar layout did not make affordances clear

  • Participants found content rows but did not tap them.
  • They often did not realise form fields needed input.
  • They did not recognise the exclamation-mark icon as the emergency tab.

The post-fieldwork build

Download on demand

Downloads resume from where they stopped on an intermittent connection, and a download is refused if the phone lacks space for it.

Content dashboard

A web dashboard where a non-technical partner adds and edits lessons as draggable blocks of text, audio, image and video, with a live preview, without a developer.

Monthly health check

An automated monthly check of the app's dependencies emails the project lead a plain-language status and asks her to forward it to whoever holds the technical contract.

Handover

Ella handed over the source code and accounts, the unmet requirements, and the changes the field work showed were needed, each recommendation given with the field observation behind it.

The receiving developer had the code running by the end of the first session and completed four starter tasks within three days. The AMEN project lead was given full access to the database and the repository.

Findings

A familiar layout did not make affordances clear

Participants found content rows but did not tap them, often did not realise form fields needed input, and did not recognise the exclamation-mark icon as the emergency tab. Local input is needed on iconography as well as content.

Continuity fails independently of documentation

In the earlier projects, what was missing was a place in the institution for access to be held.

Recorded reasoning is not enough on its own

How it is passed on, and whether it reaches the receiving developer, matter as much.
